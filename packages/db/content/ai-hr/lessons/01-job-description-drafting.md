# Job Description Drafting

A job description is often the first thing a candidate reads about your company. Most JDs fail that moment before AI ever enters the picture: they list responsibilities borrowed from the last person's title, use superlatives nobody believes, and bury the real expectations under bullet points that could describe any role at any company. AI can help you fix that — but only if you give it something worth working with.

## Start from outcomes, not a job title

The single most common mistake in JD drafting is opening a blank document and typing the role title. That pulls you straight into boilerplate. Instead, start with a conversation with the hiring manager — or a prompt to yourself — that asks: what does success look like at six months? At a year?

Outcome-framed JDs are both more honest and more useful to candidates. "Own the end-to-end offer process for 30 requisitions per quarter" tells a recruiter candidate something real. "Responsible for offer management" tells them nothing.

Once you have the outcomes written in plain language, give them to AI along with the must-have versus nice-to-have skills. That input produces something far more specific than any prompt that starts with a job title alone.

**Prompt pattern:**

> "Draft a job description for a [role] on [team]. Here are the outcomes we expect in the first six months: [list them]. Required skills: [must-haves]. Preferred but not required: [nice-to-haves]. The audience is a working professional mid-career change. Write in plain English, no superlatives, no jargon. Format: one short overview paragraph, 4-6 outcome bullets, requirements list."

Run that and you will get a draft that looks like an actual job, not a copy-paste from LinkedIn.

## Let AI do the bias scrub

Language bias in JDs is well-documented and persistent. "Rockstar," "ninja," "dominate the market," and "work hard, play hard" skew toward male applicants in measurable ways. Phrases like "recent grad preferred" or "native speaker" introduce age and national-origin signals that may be illegal in some jurisdictions. Degree requirements are often left in by habit, not necessity, closing the door to strong candidates with non-traditional backgrounds.

AI is reasonably good at surfacing these patterns because it has processed enough research and guidance to recognize them. It is not a legal compliance tool — use your legal counsel for that — but it is a fast first filter.

**Prompt pattern for bias review:**

> "Review this job description for biased or exclusionary language. Flag: gendered words or phrases, age signals, degree or credential requirements that may not be necessary for the role, any language that implies a specific cultural archetype (e.g., startup hustle culture). For each flag, explain the concern and suggest a replacement."

You will often be surprised. Phrases you have written dozens of times will get flagged, and when you read the explanation, you will see why.

## What not to put in the prompt

This matters more in HR than in most functions. Your company's internal leveling guides, salary bands, and compensation philosophy are confidential information. Do not paste them into a public AI model to help draft a JD. If you need AI to factor in level context, describe the level functionally — "this is an individual contributor role, not a manager role, mid-career, 4-6 years typical experience" — without sharing the internal document.

The same applies to identifiable information about the person you are backfilling for. If you are replacing a specific employee, do not name them in the prompt or describe their departure in ways that could identify them. Write the role forward, not backward.

## Practical quality checks before posting

AI drafts require human review on three things that AI consistently underserves:

**Legal accuracy.** AI does not know your jurisdiction's employment laws. "Equal opportunity employer" language, required disclosures, and prohibited questions vary by state and country. Never publish a JD without a legal check on the required boilerplate.

**Factual accuracy.** AI will generate specific-sounding details — reporting structures, tools, team sizes — that may not match reality. Every specific claim in the draft needs to be verified against what the manager actually told you.

**Tone consistency.** AI defaults to a slightly generic professional register. If your employer brand has a specific voice, you will likely need to make edits. That is a five-minute task once the structure is right.

## Try this

Pull one existing JD from your ATS — ideally one for a role you know well. Run the bias-review prompt on it and read what comes back. Then run the full outcome-focused drafting prompt using what you know about the role's actual deliverables.

Compare the two versions side by side: the original and the AI-assisted rewrite. Pay particular attention to the requirements section — are the credentials listed actually necessary, or are they there because they were there last time?

The goal is not to publish the AI version unchanged. It is to see, concretely, where the original JD was leaving things on the table.
