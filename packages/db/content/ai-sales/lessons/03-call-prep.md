## The 10-Minute Pre-Call Brief

Great discovery calls look spontaneous. They are not. The reps who ask the questions that stop a prospect mid-sentence — "how did you know that?" — showed up having done real preparation. The difference between a call that moves forward and one that stalls is almost always the quality of the questions, and the quality of the questions is almost always a function of preparation.

AI can cut your prep time from 30 minutes to 10 without cutting corners — if you build the right workflow.

## What Belongs in a Pre-Call Brief

A five-minute pre-call brief has five components:

1. **Company state** — current growth trajectory, recent news, the strategic moment they are in
2. **Contact background** — tenure in role, career path, any public content that reveals how they think
3. **Likely pain** — based on their industry, their role, and what you know about similar buyers
4. **Industry context** — regulatory environment, competitive dynamics, terminology they use (so you speak their language)
5. **Three discovery questions** — specific, open-ended, designed to surface the thing they have not told anyone yet

That last item is where most reps underinvest. Generic discovery questions ("What does your current process look like?") produce generic answers. Questions that show you understand their world ("I've seen a lot of ops teams in your industry struggle with [specific problem] since [specific change] — is that landing on your radar?") produce real information.

## The Pre-Call Prompt

Here is a prompt that produces a full pre-call brief in under two minutes:

```
I have a discovery call in 30 minutes with [name], [title] at [company].
Here is what I know:

Company: [paste LinkedIn company page summary or recent news snippet]
Contact: [paste their LinkedIn headline and summary]
Our product: [1-sentence description]
My goal for this call: [e.g., qualify budget, identify champion, surface primary pain]

Give me:
1. Three sentences on the company's current strategic context
2. Two things this person likely prioritizes in their role
3. The most likely objection I will hear in the first 5 minutes
4. Three discovery questions I have not seen in generic sales scripts
5. One piece of industry jargon or context that will show I know their world
```

Paste in public information only — LinkedIn profiles, company about pages, recent press releases. Do not paste in CRM notes, internal account plans, or anything that contains private company or customer data.

## Using AI to Catch What Generalists Miss

One of the highest-value uses of AI in call prep is asking it to surface context that a generalist rep would miss. If you sell into healthcare, legal, financial services, manufacturing, or any regulated or specialized industry, there is always context that matters to the buyer but requires research to find.

```
I'm selling [product] to a [title] at a company in [industry — e.g., regional community banks].
What are the top 3 regulatory or operational pressures that [title] is likely managing
right now? What language do they use to describe these pressures internally?
```

Use this to understand the landscape before you walk into it. Your buyer will know immediately whether you understand their world or whether you are learning on their time.

## What AI Cannot Prep You For

AI will tell you what the company announced publicly. It will not tell you what the VP said to his team in last Monday's QBR. It will not tell you that the CFO vetoed the last three software purchases. It will not tell you that the champion you have been nurturing just got put on a performance plan.

That information lives in your CRM, in your colleagues' call notes, and in internal Slack threads. Before any important call, check your internal records. Look at previous call notes, email threads, and any intel your manager or a colleague has on the account.

And this is where the data safety rule applies again: those internal notes are not for public models. If your company has approved Salesforce Einstein, Microsoft Copilot, or a private internal LLM, use those tools for internal-data synthesis. If not, do that synthesis manually.

## Objection Anticipation

One of the best uses of pre-call AI prep is anticipating the objection you are likely to hear early. If you can predict "we already have a solution for that" before it comes, you can prepare a response that acknowledges it rather than stumbling.

```
I'm about to talk to a [title] at a [company stage/size] company. They likely already use
[category of product]. What are the 2 most common reasons a buyer in this situation
would push back in the first call, and what is the most honest and direct way to
acknowledge each one?
```

Notice the phrase "most honest and direct." If you ask AI to give you a slick rebuttal, you will get a slick rebuttal. If you ask for an honest acknowledgment, you will get something you can actually say out loud without sounding like you are reading from a script.

## The Prep Ritual

The best sales organizations have a consistent call-prep ritual. Not every call requires 10 minutes of prep — a quick follow-up with an active champion is different from a first discovery call with a cold account. Build a tiered approach:

- **New prospect, first call:** Full five-component brief, 10 minutes
- **Follow-up call with an active deal:** Review last call notes, update on any account news, confirm the one thing you need to advance the deal
- **QBR or executive call:** Extended prep, industry context, executive-level discovery questions

## Try This

Pick a real upcoming call from your calendar this week. Set a timer for 10 minutes. Use the pre-call prompt above with public information only. Generate the five-component brief. Before the call, read through it once. After the call, note which of the three discovery questions landed and which fell flat. The pattern you see over five or six calls will tell you how to improve the prompt for your specific buyers.
