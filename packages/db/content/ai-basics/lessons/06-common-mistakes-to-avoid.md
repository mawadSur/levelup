# Common Mistakes to Avoid

Most problems people run into with AI tools at work are not exotic edge cases. They are the same handful of mistakes, repeated. This lesson names them plainly so you can recognize and avoid them.

## Treating AI output as authoritative

This is the most common mistake and the root of most of the others. It happens because AI output looks polished. The formatting is clean, the sentences are grammatical, the structure is logical. That polish creates an impression of reliability.

But as you learned in earlier lessons, the model is producing fluent text — not verified facts. The polish is a property of language generation, not of accuracy. A confidently stated falsehood looks identical to a confidently stated truth.

Treat AI output like a suggestion from a smart colleague who is opinionated, sometimes wrong, and speaking off the top of their head. Take it seriously, consider it, and check the things that matter.

## Sharing sensitive data

This bears repeating because it happens even after people know better, usually under deadline pressure.

You are running late. You need this email drafted quickly. You paste the client's full contract summary into a public AI tool because it's faster. That is the moment the mistake happens — not in the frantic hour before the deadline, but in the weeks before that when you didn't build the habit of sanitizing.

The fix is habituation, not willpower. Before anything is pasted into a public AI tool, give it a three-second scan for names, numbers, and proprietary details. Build that pause now, when stakes are low, so it is automatic when stakes are high.

## Skipping verification

Many people verify AI output on their first few uses, then stop as familiarity builds. The reasoning feels intuitive: "I've used this tool a hundred times, I have a sense of when it's reliable." The problem is that AI errors are not distributed evenly. The model can be accurate ninety-nine times and wrong in an important way on the hundredth — and the hundredth time is often not predictable from the ninety-nine.

The verification habits in lesson five are not for when you suspect the model is wrong. They are standard practice for anything where being wrong has consequences.

## Over-prompting

There is a limit to how much instruction a model can reliably follow at once. A prompt with fifteen requirements is likely to produce output that follows some and quietly drops others.

If detailed instructions are not working, the answer is not more instructions. Break the task into steps: structure first, then content, then tone. Iterating through smaller steps consistently outperforms one enormous prompt.

## Letting AI write things you don't understand

This applies most directly to technical outputs — code, legal language, financial models — but it extends further than that. If you send out a document that uses terminology you can't explain or makes arguments you can't defend, you are exposed.

Your name on a document means you are responsible for it. If someone asks you to explain or defend a point in an AI-generated document you sent, "the AI wrote it" is not a professional answer.

A useful rule: don't use AI to produce output that you could not, with a little more time, produce yourself. Use it to do faster what you know how to do. That keeps you in a position to evaluate, edit, and own the result.

## Ignoring tone — the "ChatGPT smell"

AI-generated prose has recognizable patterns. It tends to be structured to a fault — introduction, three points, conclusion. It leans on certain phrases: "it's worth noting," "it's important to remember," "in today's landscape," "delve into," "leverage," "utilize," "moving forward." The sentences are often syntactically correct but rhythmically flat.

Readers notice this even if they can't name it. Corporate AI output reads like it was written for everyone and therefore for no one.

The fix is editing. Read the draft aloud and cut filler. Replace generic phrases with words you'd actually say. Add one specific detail from your real experience. A ten-minute edit pass turns AI output into something that sounds like you.

## Forgetting that context resets

Most AI chatbots do not retain memory across conversations. The context from last week's session — your role, your project, your preferred style — is gone. Every conversation starts from zero.

For frequent tasks, keep a short prompt template that re-establishes your context. Paste it at the top of your prompt. The thirty-second investment saves the frustration of outputs that seem to have forgotten everything you established before.

## Try this

Find three examples of AI-generated text — from a document you created, from something shared in your organization, or from a public source that is openly AI-written. Read each one and mark the phrases that have the "ChatGPT smell": the generic transitions, the structured-to-a-fault paragraphs, the filler language.

Then take one of the examples and rewrite two or three sentences to remove the smell — using plain language, a specific detail, or a more natural sentence rhythm. The goal is to recognize the pattern in the wild and know how to edit through it.
