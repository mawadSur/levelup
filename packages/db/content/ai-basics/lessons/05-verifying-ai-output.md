# Verifying AI Output

Getting useful output from an AI tool is only half the job. The other half is knowing what to trust, what to check, and what to throw out. This lesson gives you a practical system for verification — one that takes a few extra minutes but protects you from the errors that do real professional damage.

## Why verification matters

When you forward an AI-generated summary to your manager, sign your name to an AI-drafted memo, or cite an AI-produced statistic in a client proposal, you are taking ownership of that content. If it is wrong, you are wrong — not the tool.

This is not theoretical risk. Attorneys have been sanctioned for submitting AI-generated briefs that cited nonexistent cases. Journalists have been embarrassed by stories that included fabricated statistics. Analysts have presented numbers to executives that turned out to be AI approximations rather than real data.

The model has no stake in the outcome. You do. Verification is the step that makes AI output yours rather than a liability you borrowed from a machine.

## The two-source rule

For any factual claim that matters — a number, a legal standard, a company's position, an industry statistic — apply the two-source rule: don't act on it until you have confirmed it from at least one source that is not the AI.

This does not mean you need to verify every sentence. It means you need to identify which sentences carry factual weight and check those.

Structural claims ("Here are three options for how to approach this") need less verification than factual claims ("According to the 2024 survey, 67% of employees reported..."). The former is a framework you can evaluate yourself. The latter is an assertion about the world that either is or isn't true.

## Citation hallucination: what it is and how to catch it

When you ask a language model for sources, it produces text that has the structure of citations. Author, year, title, journal, URL. The structure is real. The underlying paper or article may or may not be.

This is not the model lying intentionally — it is generating text that fits the expected pattern. A citation looks a certain way; the model produces something that looks that way. Whether the underlying paper exists is a separate question.

How to catch it:

1. **Click the URL.** If it 404s, leads to something unrelated, or does not exist, the citation is fabricated.
2. **Search the title directly.** Copy the article title into a search engine. If you cannot find the paper independently, treat it as unverified.
3. **Check the author.** Search the author's name plus institution. Real researchers have publication records. Made-up names often do not.
4. **Verify the claim the citation was supposed to support.** Even if the citation is real, check that the paper actually says what the AI claims it says.

## Spotting confident-but-wrong output

Some patterns appear frequently in AI output that turns out to be wrong:

**Suspiciously round numbers.** "Studies show that 70% of workers feel disengaged." Numbers that are clean multiples of five or ten deserve extra scrutiny. Real research produces messier figures — 68.4%, not 70%.

**Vague specificity.** "Research from leading universities has shown..." Which universities? Which research? Vague attribution that sounds specific is a red flag.

**No variation.** If you ask three times and get the same statistic each time, that's not reassurance — the model is consistent within itself, not consistent with external reality. Consistency in output does not prove accuracy.

**Events after the training cutoff.** If the model states something confidently about recent events — legislation passed last year, a company's current strategy, recent data — cross-check it. The model may be extrapolating or fabricating based on older patterns.

## The "would I sign this?" test

Before you send or act on AI-generated content, read it as if you wrote it yourself. Ask: if my name is on this, am I comfortable with every claim?

If you reach a sentence and think "I'm not sure that's right," that sentence needs a check. If the document overall would embarrass you professionally if a fact turned out wrong, the document needs a check.

This shifts the question from "did the AI do this correctly?" — which you cannot know in advance — to "would I personally stand behind this?" — which you can answer.

## What does not need heavy verification

Verification does not mean treating everything as suspect. Some types of AI output carry lower factual risk:

- **Structure and formatting:** reorganized text, reformatted documents, rewritten drafts.
- **Tone and clarity edits:** a rewrite that keeps your facts but improves the prose.
- **Brainstorming lists:** options you will then evaluate yourself.
- **Explanations of concepts:** things you can fact-check quickly against your own knowledge.

Save your verification energy for the output where facts matter and errors have consequences.

## Try this

Ask an AI chatbot to give you information on a topic in your field, and ask it to include sources. Take the sources it provides and look each one up independently — click URLs, search titles, look up authors.

Count how many sources you can actually verify as real and accurately described. This exercise is worth doing once with focused attention. The results will calibrate how seriously you take AI-generated citations going forward.
