# The Rules of Customer Comms

Every applicant- or borrower-facing message you send at Kapitus sits on top of a regulatory layer. You don't have to be a lawyer to write a good notice, but you do have to know what the rules are protecting and what kinds of mistakes those rules exist to prevent. AI can help you draft faster, but only if you bring that understanding to the review.

## The four laws you actually touch

Most of the comms work at Kapitus brushes up against four federal regimes. Here is what each one is really about, in the words you would use over coffee.

**TILA (Truth in Lending Act).** The borrower must know what credit actually costs. APR, fees, finance charges, payment schedules — if you change a number, you've changed a disclosure. TILA is also the home of the right to receive certain notices in writing, in plain terms, before money moves.

**RESPA (Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act).** If you touch any mortgage- or real-estate-secured product, RESPA controls escrow analyses, servicing transfer notices, error resolution, and "qualified written request" responses. The timelines are strict — five business days to acknowledge, thirty to resolve, and the format and contents are specified.

**ECOA (Equal Credit Opportunity Act) and Regulation B.** No discrimination by race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, or because someone receives public assistance. The piece you'll feel most often is the **adverse action notice**: when you decline an application, reduce a line, or change terms unfavorably, the borrower is entitled to a specific written reason within thirty days.

**FCRA (Fair Credit Reporting Act).** If a credit report informed your decision, you must say so, name the bureau, and tell the consumer they can dispute or request a copy. Risk-based pricing notices live here too.

These laws overlap. A single decline letter can be a TILA disclosure, an ECOA adverse action notice, and an FCRA risk-based pricing notice at the same time. AI does not know that. You do.

## The disclosures you must never drop

Whatever AI helps you draft, certain elements have to survive the rewrite. Treat this as your minimum checklist for any applicant- or borrower-facing comm:

- **The legal entity name** — "Kapitus LLC" or the specific Kapitus servicing entity on the contract. Not "Kapitus" alone, not "we," not a brand variant.
- **The principal reason for adverse action**, in plain English, when applicable. "Insufficient time in business" is fine; "Risk model output 0.34" is not.
- **The credit bureau's full name and contact info** when a credit report was used.
- **The consumer's right to a free copy of the report** within sixty days of the adverse action.
- **The right to dispute** the information.
- **Kapitus' contact information** for questions — name, phone, hours, address. A real path back to a human.
- **The ECOA equal-credit-opportunity clause**, when required, with the federal agency contact line.

When you feed a draft to AI to make it shorter or warmer, the first thing to lose is usually one of these. Read every output with this list in your head.

## The "would I send this to my grandmother?" test

Compliance gets you out of trouble. Tone is what keeps trust. The fastest way to check tone is to imagine the borrower is your grandmother. Would she:

- Understand every sentence on the first read?
- Know what she's supposed to do, by when, and what happens if she doesn't?
- Feel respected — not lectured, not threatened, not patronized?
- Have a clear way to reach a real person?

If any answer is no, the draft isn't done. This test is not a soft skill. Tone failures generate complaints. Complaints generate regulatory exam findings. Regulatory findings generate everything bad that follows.

## Why AI raises the bar on review, not lowers it

There is a comforting fantasy that AI will write the disclosure perfectly so reviewers can move faster. The opposite is true. AI lowers the cost of producing fluent text, so more drafts come through review, and each one carries the risk that a required element was quietly summarized away. A model will happily turn "you have the right to obtain a free copy of your consumer report from Experian within sixty days of this notice" into "you can request your credit report" — cleaner, friendlier, and now non-compliant.

What this means in practice:

- **Never let AI write the disclosure block.** Keep approved disclosure language in a snippet library and paste it verbatim. AI rewrites the surrounding letter, not the legal text.
- **Always diff the numbers.** If the input said "30 days," the output had better say "30 days." A change from thirty to thirty-five is a violation.
- **Re-read for what's missing.** The hard errors are omissions, not additions. Walk the checklist above against the final draft.

## Try this

Pull one customer comm you have sent in the last month — a decline, a payment reminder, a status update, anything. Open it next to the disclosure checklist above. Mark which items appear, which are missing, and which are present but worded loosely. Then think about how you would brief an AI tool to help you produce that letter again, faster, without losing a single one of those items. That brief — the constraints, the snippets, the required phrases — is the start of your personal prompt template for this kind of work.
