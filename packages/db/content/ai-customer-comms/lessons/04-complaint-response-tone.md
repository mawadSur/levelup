# Complaint Response Tone

A complaint response is the highest-stakes letter you will ever write at Kapitus. The borrower is already upset. The CFPB portal, the state AG's office, and the BBB are all possible next stops. The tone of your first response determines, more often than not, whether the issue escalates or resolves. AI can help you draft one. It can also produce the kind of bland corporate apology that makes everything worse.

## Why generic apologies make things worse

If you ask an AI to "write an apology email for a customer complaint," it will produce something like this:

> Dear Mr. Johnson,
>
> We sincerely apologize for the inconvenience you have experienced. Your satisfaction is our top priority, and we deeply regret any frustration this matter has caused. We value your business and appreciate your patience as we work to resolve this issue.

Read it as the borrower. Every sentence is a phrase you have heard a hundred times before — from your airline, your bank, your phone company. None of them refer to anything specific. "The inconvenience." "This matter." "This issue." The letter could be about a $5 fee or a foreclosure threat, and you would not be able to tell.

This is not a small tone issue. The CFPB explicitly looks for "boilerplate" and "non-responsive" patterns in servicer complaint responses. Regulators read these letters professionally and recognize the pattern instantly. So do borrowers — and the kind of borrower who is already filing a complaint is the kind of borrower who will recognize that you didn't actually read theirs.

## What a good first response does

The first complaint response has four jobs. In order:

**1. Show you read it.** Reference the specific complaint by date, channel, and substance. "You called on May 3 and emailed on May 5 about a $35 returned-payment fee on your April 28 payment, which you said hit your account before payday." Not "this matter." The exact thing.

**2. Take responsibility for what's actually yours.** If Kapitus made a mistake, name it. "We pulled the ACH a day earlier than your statement showed, and that's our error." If it's mixed — partly your error, partly something the borrower could have prevented — say that too. If it's not your error, you don't apologize for the substance; you acknowledge that the experience was frustrating without pretending you caused it.

**3. Say what you are doing about it.** Concrete actions, not promises to "look into it." "We've refunded the $35 fee — you'll see the credit by May 10. We've also corrected the ACH pull date on your account so this doesn't happen again next month."

**4. Offer a real next step.** A named person, a direct phone line, a deadline. Not "please don't hesitate to reach out." Maria, 1-800-555-0199 extension 234, available Monday through Friday 9 to 6 Eastern.

That structure is hard to write generically. The specifics fight the boilerplate.

## The line between sincere and admission of liability

Here is where complaint response gets careful. There is real legal daylight between an empathetic, accountable letter and one that admits liability in a way that could be used against Kapitus in litigation, arbitration, or a regulatory action.

You can say: "We made a mistake. We pulled the payment a day early."

You should not say: "We violated our agreement with you." That is a legal conclusion, and you are not the one who gets to make it.

You can say: "We understand this caused real problems for your business."

You should not say: "We caused $4,000 in damages to your business." That is a quantified admission. Unless your legal team has approved that number, do not put it in writing.

You can say: "We refunded the fee and corrected the underlying error so this won't happen again."

You should not say: "This was a violation of TILA / RESPA / state lending law." Even when you suspect it might be, that determination is for compliance and counsel.

AI does not know this distinction. It will happily generate either column. Your job, on review, is to keep the empathy and accountability on the left side and pull anything that drifts to the right side.

## Using AI to draft, not to decide

The role for AI in complaint response is to handle the first 80% of the draft so you can spend your time on the 20% that matters. A workable prompt:

> Draft a first response to the customer complaint below. Use these facts only — do not invent any details. Structure: (1) acknowledge the specific complaint, (2) take responsibility for the parts that are ours, (3) state the concrete actions we are taking, (4) give a named contact and deadline. Plain English, no boilerplate apologies, no phrases like "your satisfaction is our top priority" or "we apologize for the inconvenience." Do not characterize anything as a violation of law or contract. Do not quantify damages.

You then review for the legal-conclusion trap, the quantified-admission trap, and the boilerplate trap. You add the human details only you know — the borrower called Maria personally and was kind on the phone, or this is the third complaint from this account, or compliance has flagged the file for closer review. Those facts shape tone in ways the model can't see.

## When to slow down

Some complaints should never get an AI-assisted same-day response.

- Anything alleging discrimination — ECOA, fair lending, disparate treatment.
- Anything alleging fraud or identity theft.
- Anything filed through a regulator (CFPB, state AG, state DFI).
- Anything that names litigation, lawyers, or "I will be reporting this to."
- Anything where the borrower is in active financial distress and the complaint touches collections behavior.

These go to compliance and legal first, with the AI draft attached as a starting point only. Speed is your friend on a billing dispute. On a fair-lending allegation, careful is the only speed.

## Try this

Pull the last three complaint responses your team sent. Read each one out loud — actually out loud, slowly. Mark every phrase that could have been written by anyone, about anything ("we apologize for the inconvenience," "your satisfaction is our top priority," "we appreciate your patience"). The percentage of the letter that survives the read-aloud test is your real signal-to-noise ratio. Then think about which of those generic phrases you would have written if AI hadn't suggested them. That's the habit you're trying to unlearn.
