## The Volume Problem in Copy

Every experienced copywriter knows the first headline is almost never the best one. The best one is usually somewhere between draft 8 and draft 15, after you have worked past the obvious framings into something unexpected that still lands. The problem is time. Writing 15 headline variants by hand takes an hour. With AI, it takes three minutes.

This is where AI adds genuine value in marketing: not because it writes better than you, but because it removes the cost of volume. When generating 30 variants costs nothing, you stop settling for the third idea.

## "AI Smell" in Copy: What to Cut

AI-generated copy has a recognizable fingerprint. Buyers and readers have learned to feel it, even if they cannot name it. When you see these phrases in AI output, delete them before you do anything else:

- "leverage" (as a verb)
- "synergy" and its relatives
- "transform" and "transformative"
- "supercharge"
- "best-in-class"
- "seamless" and "frictionless"
- "empower" and "enable"
- "game-changer"
- "cutting-edge" and "state-of-the-art"
- Any sentence that starts with "In today's fast-paced world..."

These are not just clichés. They are signals that the copy was not written for anyone in particular. Good copy is specific. Specific copy names a person, a pain, a situation. The phrases above name nothing.

## The Headline Prompt That Works

The key to useful headline generation is specificity in the prompt. Generic prompt, generic output. The prompt below consistently produces usable material:

```
Generate 12 headlines for {feature or product} aimed at {specific audience description}.

Constraints:
- 8 words or fewer per headline
- No marketing clichés (no "leverage", "transform", "seamless", "best-in-class", or similar)
- Each headline must lead with the customer's specific pain or their desired state — not the product
- Write in plain, direct English
- No punctuation gimmicks
```

Read through the 12. Two or three will be obviously wrong for your brand. Three or four will be mediocre. Two or three will be genuinely interesting. Circle those and work from them.

## The "Five Grandmother" Test

Before any headline or piece of copy goes further in your process, apply this test: would five non-marketers — people who have never worked in a marketing department — understand exactly what this is for and why they should care?

This is not about dumbing copy down. It is about stripping out the assumption that your reader already knows what you do and why it matters. Jargon is not a problem for your reader because they are not smart enough. It is a problem because it forces them to do interpretive work that you should have done for them.

Run the test literally. Send the headline to someone outside marketing. Ask them what they think the product does. If their answer is vague or wrong, the headline is not doing its job, regardless of how clever you found it.

## Adapting Across Channels

The same positioning generates different copy for different placements. A homepage H1 has to work in two seconds. A LinkedIn ad needs a hook before "see more." An email subject line competes with 40 others. AI can produce channel-adapted variants quickly:

```
Take this core message: {your working message}

Rewrite it as:
1. A homepage H1 (≤8 words, no verb "to be")
2. A LinkedIn ad first line (≤15 words, must name a pain or create curiosity)
3. An email subject line (≤7 words, no clickbait)
4. A conference booth sign (≤6 words, visible at 10 feet)

Keep the same underlying claim across all four. Adjust only the format.
```

Channel adaptation is not the same as repositioning every time you change format. The claim stays consistent; the frame changes.

## Iteration Is the Point

The trap with AI copy generation is stopping at the first round. The prompt generates 12 headlines. You pick one. You ship it. That is better than nothing, but it is not iteration.

Real iteration looks like this: run the first prompt, pick the two most interesting outputs, then run a second prompt asking AI to push each of those two further. Ask for more specificity, a more surprising angle, or a version that leads with the outcome instead of the pain. You are directing a revision process, not accepting a first draft.

```
This headline is working: "{best headline from round one}"
It is working because: {what you think makes it strong}
Now generate 6 variants that push this further.
Direction: make the outcome even more specific, or lead with a more unexpected angle.
Same constraints: ≤8 words, no clichés, no product-first framing.
```

The second round is almost always stronger than the first.

## Try This

Take a real headline you wrote — from your current homepage, a recent campaign, or an upcoming launch. Run the generation prompt above. Get 12 variants. Pick the two that feel strongest and run the iteration prompt to push further. Take the single best result and rewrite it in your own voice, tightening any word that is not doing work. Run it past the five grandmother test. Compare the final version to what you started with. Note how many rounds it took to get there — that number is your benchmark for future sessions.
