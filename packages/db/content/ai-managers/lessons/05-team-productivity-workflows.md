## The Mandate That Does Not Work

"Everyone should start using AI" is one of the least useful things you can say to your team. It puts the burden of figuring out how on each individual and guarantees inconsistent results. Six weeks later, three people are using AI constantly, four tried it twice and gave up, and two are skeptical but will not say so.

The mandate fails because it skips the step where you identify specific, repeated tasks where AI delivers real value in your team's actual work. Those tasks are high-effort, low-creativity, and structurally repetitive. Those are the targets. "Use AI" is not a target.

## Three High-Leverage Workflows

Most teams have dozens of repeated tasks. Start with three that consistently yield results:

### Meeting prep

Before a significant meeting — a stakeholder update, a difficult 1:1, a project kickoff — there is always preparation work: reviewing what happened last time, gathering relevant context, drafting an agenda. This is the kind of structured, information-processing task where AI reliably adds value.

A template for your team:

```
I have a [type] meeting with [stakeholder/team] on [date].
Context from our last meeting: [paste notes or key points]
My goals for this meeting: [list 2-3]

Draft an agenda in 5-7 bullet points.
Flag any unresolved items from last time I should address.
Suggest one clarifying question I should ask.
```

The person running the meeting fills in the brackets. The AI handles the drafting. The meeting owner reviews and adjusts. Total time: 5 minutes instead of 20.

### Status updates

Status updates are the task most people spend more time on than the content warrants. Getting the level of detail right, the tone right, the length right — for a different audience each time — is genuinely tedious.

Build a template:

```
Write a [audience: exec/peer/stakeholder] status update for [project name].
This week: [bullet your actual updates]
Next week: [what you're doing next]
Blockers: [any blockers, or "none"]

Keep it to [3-4 sentences / a short paragraph / a half-page brief].
Use a factual, direct tone — no spin.
```

The format adjusts by audience. The person still provides the substance. AI produces the right packaging for the audience without the person having to think about format.

### Async decisions

Many decisions do not require a meeting — they require enough information to be presented clearly so people can respond asynchronously. The reason they end up in meetings is that writing a clear decision request takes effort, and it is easier to just schedule something.

Template:

```
I need a decision from [stakeholders] on [topic].
Background they need: [key context]
Options: A: [describe]. B: [describe].
My recommendation: [which option and why — optional]
I need a decision by [date].

Draft a concise async decision request I can send via [Slack/email].
Keep it under 200 words. End with a clear call to action.
```

When your team has access to these templates, the default shifts from "schedule a meeting" to "write an async request." That shift compounds over months.

## Building a Team Prompt Library

A prompt library is a shared document — a wiki page, a Notion doc, a pinned Slack post — of prompts that work for your team's specific work. The difference between a prompt library and a list of generic AI tips is that the prompts in your library have been tested on your team's actual tasks and adjusted based on what worked.

Start small. After someone on your team gets a genuinely useful result from AI, ask them to paste the prompt they used. Spend five minutes at your next team meeting reviewing two or three entries. Over a quarter, you end up with a living document of institutional knowledge about how to use AI for your team's specific context.

The library also normalizes sharing failures. Prompts that did not work, AI outputs that were confidently wrong, tasks where AI turned out to be slower than just doing it — these belong in the library too. Teams that only share wins develop a false sense of reliability.

## Running AI Wins and Fails in Retros

Add a standing retro item: "What did you try with AI this sprint? What worked and what did not?" It surfaces experimentation you would not hear about otherwise, normalizes both use and skepticism, and creates peer learning. Someone who got a great result from an unusual prompt is now teaching the team.

The framing matters: "report your AI usage" drives compliance. "Share what you learned" drives capability. The goal is teams that improve their judgment about when AI helps and when it does not — not teams with high usage numbers.

## Try This

Pick one task that happens repeatedly on your team — status updates, meeting agendas, project briefs, whatever takes the most time relative to its strategic importance. Design the AI workflow for that specific task: write the template, identify what the person needs to fill in, decide what level of review is required before sending. Then bring it to the next team meeting. Build it together. Five people improving the same template in a 20-minute conversation will produce something better than what any one person would have built alone — and the team will actually use something they helped design.
