## Your Team Watches What You Do

The fastest way to set AI norms on your team is not a policy document. It is your own visible behavior.

If you use AI and never mention it, you send one signal. If you say "I had AI draft this and then rewrote the structure" in a team meeting, you send a completely different one. If you catch an AI error in a document you circulated, own it: "I used AI here and should have caught this." Your team calibrates against what they observe, not what you tell them to do.

This means modeling failure, not just success. Managers who only mention AI when it went well create a culture where people hide AI-assisted errors. Managers who talk honestly about both — "this was useful" and "I should not have trusted that number" — create a culture where catching mistakes is rewarded.

## Setting Norms Without Banning

Banning AI does not stop AI use. It stops visible AI use. People will keep using whatever tools help them get work done. If you ban tools without providing alternatives, you push that use underground, where you have no visibility, no quality standards, and no ability to help when something goes wrong.

The productive alternative is governed use: clear guidance on which tools are approved, what data can go into them, when human review is required, and how to escalate when uncertain.

A workable starting framework:

**Approved tools.** Name the specific tools your team can use. This removes ambiguity and prevents people from defaulting to whatever is most convenient, which may not be compliant.

**Data classification.** Be explicit about what data can go into AI tools. Public information and internal-but-non-sensitive drafts are typically fine. HR data, customer PII, financial projections, IP, and competitive information typically are not. Your company may have data classification standards you can reference; if not, use common sense and document it.

**Human review requirements.** For which outputs must a human verify the content before it leaves the team? Any external communication, any document with numbers or factual claims, any HR-adjacent content — these should require explicit review. AI-generated content that goes out without review is where quality problems accumulate.

**Escalation path.** When someone is unsure whether a given use is appropriate, who do they ask? Name a person or a process. "I wasn't sure so I didn't ask" is a failure mode you want to close.

## What to Watch For

Managing AI use in practice means recognizing specific warning signs:

**Unedited AI prose in external communications.** Most AI-generated text has a detectable pattern — a certain hedging quality, a tendency to start paragraphs with "It is important to note that," a particular brand of bullet point structure. When you start seeing this pattern in messages your team sends to customers or senior leadership, it means the review step is being skipped. Address the process, not the person.

**Fabricated numbers in reports or dashboards.** AI will confidently produce statistics. Some of those statistics do not come from data you provided — they come from the model's training patterns. A number in a management report or a dashboard that no one can trace to a source is a red flag. Require that every number have a documented source before it appears in anything consequential.

**Hallucinated citations or references.** AI frequently invents citations — paper titles, authors, URLs, case studies — that sound authoritative and do not exist. If your team is producing documents with external references, those references need to be verified against real sources.

**Slop in code reviews.** Code generated by AI is often syntactically correct and logically wrong. When reviewers start rubber-stamping AI-generated code because it passes surface-level checks, technical debt accumulates silently. Make sure your code review standards explicitly apply to AI-generated code.

**The confidence gap.** AI-generated outputs often sound more confident than the underlying analysis warrants. A two-sentence AI summary of a complex situation will not include the caveats a careful human writer would include. Remind your team that AI output sounds authoritative even when it is wrong, and train them to add their own caveats rather than deferring to the model's tone.

## Transparency With Your Own Manager

Model the transparency you want from your team not just with your reports, but upward. If you used AI to draft the Q3 planning doc, be comfortable saying so if asked. The standard is simple: are you confident this is accurate and represents your actual thinking? If yes, it does not matter how you got there. If the answer is "I am not actually sure" — that is when transparency matters most, because that is when errors get escalated rather than hidden.

## Try This

Write your team's AI norms in exactly five bullets. Not a policy document — five clear, specific sentences your team can read in 30 seconds and actually remember. What tools are approved. What data stays out. When review is required. What good looks like. And what you do when you are unsure.

Share them in your next team meeting. Ask one question: "What did I miss that would help you?" Listen to the answers. The gaps your team identifies will tell you more about how AI is actually being used on your team than anything else you could do this quarter.
