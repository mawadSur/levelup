# The Loan Officer and AI

You spend your day at the edge of Kapitus. Applicants email you their paystubs at 11pm. Underwriting bounces a file back asking for one more bank statement. A merchant calls confused about why their offer changed. Five of those conversations are happening at once, and somewhere in the middle of all that, you're being told to "use AI to be more efficient."

This path is about doing that well — without leaking applicant data, without writing a decline letter that ends up in front of a regulator, and without letting a chatbot make decisions it has no business making.

## Where AI actually helps a loan officer

Be specific about this. The honest list is shorter than the marketing suggests.

- **Drafting outbound communication.** Follow-up emails to applicants who are missing a document. Status updates. Polite nudges. AI is genuinely good at producing a clear, professional first draft you can edit in twenty seconds instead of writing from scratch in five minutes.
- **Rewriting for tone.** You wrote something terse because you were rushed. Paste it in, ask for "warmer, still professional, two sentences shorter." Out comes a version you can send.
- **Summarizing documents you understand.** A merchant sends 18 pages of bank statements. You already know what underwriting needs. AI can pull "average daily balance, lowest balance, count of NSF events" out of the raw text far faster than you can read line by line.
- **Translating jargon into plain language.** An applicant asks why their offer dropped. You know it's because of seasonal revenue volatility. AI can help you say that in a sentence the merchant will actually understand.
- **Comparing two drafts.** You wrote a decline letter one way and a coworker wrote it another. Paste both, ask which is clearer and why. The model is a competent second reader.

None of these are decisions. They are all drafting, summarizing, or rewording — tasks where the underlying judgment is already yours.

## Where AI actively hurts a loan officer

This list is shorter but more important.

- **It does not decide eligibility.** Ever. If you ever find yourself typing "should we approve this applicant" into a chatbot, stop. That is underwriting's call, run against Kapitus's policies and bureau data the model does not have. The model will give you a confident answer anyway, because it always gives a confident answer. That answer is worthless and exposing the applicant's data to produce it is a real harm.
- **It hallucinates loan terms.** Ask a general-purpose chatbot "what's the standard prepayment penalty on a 12-month MCA" and it will invent a plausible-sounding number that is not Kapitus's number. The only source of truth for Kapitus product terms is the Kapitus product sheet. Treat anything else as fiction.
- **It fakes citations and regulations.** "Per Regulation B section 1002.9(b)(2)..." The model is excellent at producing text that looks like a legal citation. The citation may be real, paraphrased wrong, or invented. You cannot send a decline letter relying on an AI-generated regulatory reference without verifying it.
- **It cheerfully accepts applicant data you should never paste.** A general-purpose chatbot sitting outside Kapitus's stack is not a system of record. Pasting an SSN, full bank statement, driver's license number, or anything else identifying into one is a data exposure event. Your manager will hear about it. So will compliance.

## The mental model: drafting assistant, never decision-maker

If you take one thing from this path, take this. Every interaction with AI in your job should fit the sentence:

> _"I have already decided what to do; I'm using AI to help me say it, format it, or extract it faster."_

Approve the applicant? Your call, with underwriting. The AI helps you write the welcome email.

Decline the applicant? Your call, with underwriting and Kapitus's adverse-action process. The AI helps you draft the letter — and then you check it against ECOA rules before it goes out.

Tell the applicant they need more documents? Your call. The AI helps you write a polite, specific request.

When the order is reversed — when the AI is making the call and you're rubber-stamping it — you're outside what the tool is for. And you're putting Kapitus, the applicant, and your own license on the hook for a decision made by a system that does not understand what a UCC filing is and cannot tell you why it said what it said.

## A word on what you've already learned

You've completed AI Basics. You know the model is generating plausible text, not retrieving facts. You know fluency is not accuracy. You know to verify anything that matters.

You've completed Kapitus Foundations. You know our products, our underwriting flow, and our compliance posture.

This path puts those two together. Every lesson from here on is a loan-officer-specific application of those two foundations: how to draft applicant emails without exposing data, how to write decline letters without violating ECOA, how to summarize documents safely, and how to chain it all together across one real applicant relationship.

## Try this

Open whatever AI tool you use most. Without typing anything yet, write down three tasks from your last shift where AI could have helped — and three where you would have gotten yourself in trouble if you'd used it.

The list will probably look something like: _helpful — drafting a follow-up email, summarizing a paystub, rewriting a stiff message. risky — deciding whether to approve a borderline applicant, generating a decline letter without checking the language, pasting an SSN to ask "is this number real."_

Keep that list. The rest of this path is about making the helpful column bigger and the risky column smaller.
