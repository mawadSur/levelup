# Drafting Support Replies with AI

The hardest part of a busy queue is not knowing what to say — it is having the time and energy to say it well, ticket after ticket. AI drafting removes the blank-page problem. You stop starting from nothing and start editing instead, which is faster and usually produces a better result.

This lesson covers a reliable structure for drafting, how to prompt for it, and where you must always add the human layer before sending.

## The four-part reply structure

Every good support reply does four things in order:

1. **Acknowledge** — Show the customer you understood their specific problem and how it made them feel. One sentence, genuine, no generic openers.
2. **Answer** — Solve the problem or explain what needs to happen next, in plain language. No jargon, no acronyms that only your team uses.
3. **Next step** — Tell them exactly what happens now: what you will do, what they need to do, and when they can expect to hear from you.
4. **Check in** — Ask one brief question to confirm the reply landed correctly. This is the single most underused move in support writing.

This pattern works whether you are handling a billing dispute, a technical failure, or a feature question. It scales across tones from urgent to casual. And it maps cleanly onto what you want AI to generate.

## The drafting prompt

Here is a prompt that produces usable first drafts:

> Draft a reply to a customer who reports {issue}. They are feeling {feeling}. Include: a specific acknowledgement of their situation, the answer in plain language, the next step they or we need to take, and a check-in question. Keep it under 120 words. No corporate phrases.

The brackets are placeholders you fill in before submitting. "The order did not arrive after five business days" is more useful than "a shipping problem." "Frustrated and anxious" is more useful than "upset." The more specific your prompt, the less editing you will need to do.

You can also front-load context. If the customer sent you a three-paragraph message, paste the key sentences from it directly into your prompt rather than paraphrasing. The model will mirror the customer's vocabulary, which makes the reply feel less canned.

## What AI produces well

AI is reliable at structure. When you give it the four-part pattern, it will generally produce replies that hit all four parts without leaving gaps. It is also reliable at neutral, clear language — it defaults away from technical jargon unless you ask for it.

AI is serviceable at tone. If you tell it the customer is frustrated, it will soften the language and lead with empathy. It will not know that this is the third time the customer has contacted you in a week, or that your company made an error, or that the customer's name is not in the ticket subject line — but it will get the emotional direction right.

## What you must always add yourself

**The customer's name.** If you have it, use it. AI will not add it unless you put it in the prompt, and even then it may place it awkwardly.

**Order numbers, ticket IDs, timestamps, error codes.** These are the specifics that prove you read the ticket. Paste the real ones in. Do not let AI invent them — it will if you give it enough room.

**A real apology when one is warranted.** If your company made an error, the apology needs to come from a person. "I apologize for the confusion" generated by AI and pasted without modification feels different to a customer than a sentence you wrote because you meant it.

**Data privacy.** Do not paste a customer's full name, email, phone number, billing details, or account credentials into a public AI tool. Use your company's approved internal AI tool if one is available. If you are unsure which tools are approved, ask your team lead before you paste anything.

## Where AI drafts go wrong

The most common failure is over-politeness. AI defaults to phrases like "I appreciate your patience," "please do not hesitate to reach out," and "I hope this helps." These phrases have been used so many times that they communicate almost nothing. Read every draft you get and remove the filler before you send.

The second failure is vagueness on the next step. AI will write "we will look into this" when the customer needs to know "I will escalate this to our billing team and you will receive an email by 5 p.m. tomorrow." Replace the vague with the specific every time.

## Try this

Pull a real ticket from your queue — one you have not replied to yet. Write a prompt using the format above. Generate two drafts with slightly different feeling descriptors (for example, "frustrated" and "anxious"). Compare them. Pick the one that sounds more like you, and rewrite the final sentence so the closing is in your voice. Note how long the full process took. For most agents, it is faster than writing from scratch, and the result is more complete.

Do this with five tickets before moving to the next lesson. The goal is to build the habit until the prompt-then-edit loop becomes automatic.
